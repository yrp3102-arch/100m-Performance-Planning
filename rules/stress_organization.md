# Stress Organization Rules

Version: 0.1
Status: Draft
Date: 2026-09-15

## Purpose

This document defines the conditions under which selected training stimuli can
coexist across time without undermining priority, exposure quality, recovery,
tissue tolerance, or competition expression.

It operates after resource priority and candidate intervention representation.

The governing relationship is:

Priority Allocation

→ Selected Candidate Interventions

→ Stimulus, Dose, Cost, and Risk Profiles

→ Stress Organization

→ Executable Plan Structure.

Stress Organization answers:

> How should selected stimuli be clustered, separated, sequenced, and bounded
> so that their combined demands remain compatible with the plan's priorities
> and constraints?

It does not decide the athlete's performance problem, choose a training-method
library, prescribe fixed weekly layouts, or implement response-driven plan
revision.

---

## 1. Stress Is Context-Dependent

Training stress is the demand and cost produced by an actual exposure in
relation to the athlete's current state and surrounding exposures.

It is not a fixed property of an exercise name.

The stress attributed to an intervention depends on:

- actual dose;
- achieved intensity and quality;
- execution strategy;
- familiarity and training history;
- current fatigue and recovery state;
- tissue status and exposure tolerance;
- surface, equipment, and environment;
- within-session placement;
- proximity to other exposures;
- accumulated load;
- and competition timing.

The same prescribed task can produce different stress profiles across athletes,
sessions, and phases.

The same athlete can experience different costs from nominally identical work
when state or context changes.

Stress Organization must therefore use the represented intervention and
expected demand profile from the
[Stimulus-Dose-Cost Core Model](../core_models/03_stimulus_dose_cost_model.md),
then remain open to correction by actual exposure and observed response.

Organization is conditional.

No arrangement is universally optimal independent of athlete, priority, dose,
constraints, and time.

---

## 2. Stress Is Multidimensional

Stress must be represented as a profile rather than a single undifferentiated
load score.

Decision-relevant dimensions may include:

| Stress dimension | Object of concern | Main organization question |
|---|---|---|
| High-output or neuromuscular demand | Ability to express rapid, coordinated output | Will one exposure reduce the quality or availability required by another? |
| Mechanical demand | Magnitude, rate, direction, and repetition of force | Do exposures create compatible or excessive force demands? |
| Local tissue stress | Site-specific loading and tolerance | Do different tasks accumulate stress in the same tissue despite different labels? |
| Metabolic demand | Energetic disturbance and fatigue | Will residual metabolic cost alter quality, recovery, or later expression? |
| Technical demand | Stability, precision, and coordination under task conditions | Does earlier or concurrent work disrupt the organization the priority task requires? |
| Recovery demand | Time and resources required for relevant restoration | Is there a plausible recovery opportunity before the next dependent exposure? |
| Psychological and attentional demand | Focus, arousal, learning, and decision effort | Does complexity or concentration reduce execution quality elsewhere? |
| Competition-expression demand | Freshness, readiness, logistics, and race execution | Does training cost impair the ability to express performance when required? |

These dimensions can overlap without being interchangeable.

A task can be low in one dimension and high in another.

Low heart rate, short duration, low subjective effort, or small total volume
does not prove low tissue, technical, or high-output cost.

The system must not compress distinct stress dimensions into an unsupported
universal total when that aggregation would hide a consequential local demand.

Qualitative profiles are permitted when measurement precision is limited.

The precision of the profile must match the evidence.

## 3. Actual Demand Has Priority Over Exercise Label

Exercise labels support communication but do not establish actual stress.

The system must distinguish:

Prescribed Category

from

Actual Exposure

from

Realized Demand and Response.

A session labeled `High`, `Low`, `Recovery`, `Technical`, or `Strength` must be
organized according to what it actually requires and produces.

When label and represented demand conflict, the demand profile governs the
stress interpretation.

Relevant evidence includes:

- completed rather than planned dose;
- achieved velocity, load, duration, density, and execution quality;
- local tissue loading;
- residual response;
- athlete-specific tolerance;
- and the effect on the next priority exposure.

Actual demand can differ from intended demand because of execution, fatigue,
environment, task substitution, or dose drift.

That mismatch belongs in the feedback record and may change future
organization.

It does not retroactively make the original label an adequate description.

## 4. Clustering and Separation

Clustering places exposures with overlapping or high demands near one another
to concentrate stress and preserve lower-demand periods elsewhere.

Separation distributes exposures to protect local quality, reduce combined
dose, or respect athlete-specific tolerance.

Neither arrangement is a default law.

### 4.1 Conditions Supporting Clustering

Clustering is more defensible when:

- the exposures require compatible athlete states or facilities;
- their sequence preserves the quality of the Primary work;
- the combined session demand remains tolerable;
- the same-day concentration creates a real recovery opportunity afterward;
- additional transitions or separate days would create greater total cost;
- and local tissue overlap does not exceed current tolerance.

Clustering may support:

- protection of lower-demand periods;
- consolidation of warm-up or facility cost;
- clearer contrast between demanding and restorative opportunities;
- or coordination of stimuli with compatible temporal effects.

Its potential benefit does not prove that all demanding work belongs together.

### 4.2 Conditions Supporting Separation

Separation is more defensible when:

- one exposure would materially reduce the quality of another;
- combined dose exceeds session tolerance;
- local tissue stress becomes excessive when concentrated;
- technical learning requires a less fatigued state;
- attention or time limits prevent adequate execution;
- the athlete has poor tolerance for long or dense sessions;
- or distinct exposures require incompatible states or environments.

Separation may protect local quality while reducing the size of uninterrupted
recovery periods.

That trade-off must remain visible.

### 4.3 Clustering Boundary

A cluster is justified only if the full cluster remains feasible.

The system must evaluate:

- order-dependent quality;
- combined dose;
- overlapping stress dimensions;
- residual cost;
- session duration and attention;
- and the actual demand of the following period.

Clustering is not successful merely because all prescribed tasks were
completed.

If later tasks consistently lose the required quality or the intended recovery
period remains costly, the cluster is not producing its stated organizational
benefit.

## 5. Recovery Opportunity

A recovery opportunity is a period whose planned demands permit sufficient
restoration or compensation for the next decision-relevant exposure.

It is not defined by calendar emptiness or a day label alone.

The required recovery relationship depends on:

- the prior actual exposure;
- the stress dimensions involved;
- athlete-specific response history;
- tissue status;
- familiarity;
- sleep, nutrition, travel, work, and life stress;
- the quality required by the next task;
- and the consequence of incomplete recovery.

Recovery need is target-specific.

An athlete may be able to perform one task while remaining unready for another
that depends on a different tissue, capability, or quality state.

Subjective freshness does not prove complete restoration of all relevant
systems.

Likewise, an imperfect readiness indicator does not prove that every possible
task is inappropriate.

The plan should preserve recovery opportunities for Primary exposures and
competition expression before allocating optional work into them.

A missed exposure should not automatically be compressed into a recovery
opportunity simply to preserve calendar completion.

## 6. Tissue Stress Overlap

Tissue stress must be organized by the structures and loading characteristics
involved, not only by exercise category.

Different tasks can overlap through:

- the same local tissue;
- similar loading direction;
- high force or loading rate;
- repeated contacts;
- long muscle-length loading;
- acceleration or deceleration demand;
- unfamiliar range or surface;
- and insufficient restoration between exposures.

A small global training load can coexist with a large local tissue demand.

The system should treat meaningful local symptoms, tolerance limits, and
medical or rehabilitation restrictions as constraints on organization.

General readiness or low metabolic demand must not override a relevant local
tissue warning.

When two selected interventions load the same tissue, their combined demand
must be evaluated even if they occur under different labels or in different
sessions.

Separation can reduce acute concentration without necessarily reducing total
weekly tissue demand.

Clustering can create longer intervals without guaranteeing that the
concentrated exposure is tolerable.

Neither spacing nor clustering substitutes for appropriate dose.

The [Constraint Handling Rules](./constraint_handling.md) govern hard tissue,
medical, safety, and environmental boundaries.

## 7. Quality Protection

Quality protection preserves the execution conditions required for an intended
stimulus or competition expression.

Quality is task-specific.

It may refer to:

- velocity or rapid output;
- technical stability;
- force or power expression;
- precision and learning;
- completion under intended fatigue conditions;
- or another represented exposure criterion.

Quality-dominant Primary work should be placed where the athlete has a
plausible opportunity to achieve its required state and execution.

Earlier work is incompatible when its expected or observed cost prevents the
Primary exposure from meeting the conditions that give it its intended
identity.

Supporting work may precede Primary work only when:

- its cost is sufficiently low;
- its effect is compatible with the required quality;
- the sequence has an athlete-specific or task-specific rationale;
- and the benefit exceeds the risk of degrading the Primary exposure.

An acute enhancement hypothesis does not by itself establish long-term
superiority of the sequence.

When quality loss is part of an intended stimulus, the acceptable relationship
must be defined in advance.

Unplanned collapse must not be relabeled as successful fatigue training after
the fact.

Completion volume is subordinate to the exposure quality needed for the
current objective.

---

## 8. Sequencing

Sequencing governs how the order of selected exposures changes their realized
quality, stimulus, cost, and interpretability.

A sequence is justified relative to:

- priority rank;
- required entry state;
- compatibility of intended stimuli;
- effect of earlier work on later quality;
- local tissue and recovery demand;
- technical and attentional requirements;
- transition and setup cost;
- and the value of information produced by the arrangement.

The default organizational claim is that higher-priority quality-dependent work
should receive the state it requires.

This does not establish one universal exercise order.

A lower-priority exposure may appear earlier when it is a necessary preparation
for the Primary work and its expected benefit exceeds its cost.

A later exposure must not be counted as successfully delivered merely because
its task label remained in the session.

If the earlier exposure changes dose, intensity, execution, or athlete state,
the identity and value of the later exposure may also change.

Across sessions, sequence should preserve the dependencies among exposure,
recovery, adaptation, and competition expression.

Across longer periods, sequencing should not be inferred from calendar blocks
alone; the relevant adaptation and constraint relationships must remain visible.

## 9. Interference

Interference exists when one exposure reduces the quality, adaptation,
recovery, availability, or competition expression associated with another.

Stress Organization must distinguish at least:

- acute execution interference;
- residual recovery interference;
- local tissue overlap;
- technical interference;
- adaptation-level interference;
- scheduling and facility interference;
- attentional or learning interference;
- and opportunity-cost interference.

These forms can coexist.

The absence of a proven molecular interference mechanism does not establish
that two interventions coexist without practical cost.

An intervention can impair the plan by consuming time, sleep, recovery,
attention, or high-quality exposure capacity even when long-term physiological
interference is uncertain.

Likewise, concurrent training is not automatically interfering merely because
two labels differ.

An interference claim should identify:

- the exposures involved;
- the affected outcome;
- the likely shared or competing demand;
- the relevant timing;
- the supporting observation or prior;
- and uncertainty.

When interference cannot be identified precisely, the system should preserve
the candidate explanations rather than assign all decline to generic fatigue.

## 10. Residual Cost

Residual cost is the portion of an exposure's demand that remains relevant to a
later task, recovery opportunity, or competition.

Stress Organization is forward-looking.

The value of an exposure depends partly on what state it leaves for what comes
next.

Residual cost may involve:

- reduced output;
- local tissue sensitivity;
- soreness or muscle damage;
- metabolic disturbance;
- technical instability;
- attentional fatigue;
- sleep or schedule disruption;
- or reduced competition freshness.

Residual effects do not share a universal time course.

Observation at a fixed time point is an opportunity to update the state
estimate, not proof that recovery is complete or incomplete.

Spacing should reflect:

- actual prior dose;
- the specific residual dimension;
- individual response history;
- comparability of observations;
- the demand of the next exposure;
- and the consequence of being wrong.

No universal 24-hour, 48-hour, or 72-hour rule follows from the model.

When cost is uncertain, the plan should avoid treating the missing value as
zero.

## 11. Competition as Stress

Competition is an actual exposure with performance, technical, psychological,
logistical, tissue, and recovery demands.

It must not be added to an otherwise unchanged training structure as if it were
external to load organization.

Competition demand can include:

- warm-up and call-room requirements;
- acceleration and high-velocity exposure;
- rounds or repeated races;
- maximal execution intent;
- travel and schedule disruption;
- environmental conditions;
- psychological arousal;
- and post-competition residual cost.

The actual competition profile depends on what occurred, not only on the
planned race schedule.

Competition can:

- satisfy part of a priority exposure;
- create an additional high-demand cluster;
- replace a planned stimulus;
- reduce the value of nearby supporting work;
- alter recovery opportunity;
- or change the balance from development toward expression.

Competition importance and proximity increase the consequence of residual
cost that masks performance expression.

This Rule does not prescribe taper magnitude or final-session content.

It requires competition to remain inside the stress model.

## 12. High / Low Organization

High / Low is an optional organization strategy that clusters relatively high-
demand exposures and protects relatively lower-demand periods between them.

It is not a universal biological law, fixed weekly template, or proof that all
stress belongs to one high-low axis.

### 12.1 High Classification

`High` describes an exposure or cluster with meaningful demand in one or more
dimensions relevant to later quality or recovery.

High classification should identify which dimensions are high and for whom.

It must not rely on the task name or a generic claim of central nervous system
fatigue.

Different High exposures may create different local and residual costs.

They are not automatically interchangeable or compatible.

### 12.2 Low Classification

`Low` describes a period whose actual demands are sufficiently small relative
to the athlete's state and the next priority exposure to preserve its intended
recovery function.

Low does not mean zero activity or zero cost.

A nominally easy session can fail to be Low through excessive volume, repeated
contacts, local tissue stress, novelty, density, duration, technical demand, or
competition with sleep and recovery.

The next priority exposure and relevant tissue response help determine whether
the Low classification was achieved.

No single heart-rate zone, exercise list, or session duration defines Low for
all athletes and contexts.

### 12.3 Conditional Use

High / Low organization is more defensible when:

- demanding exposures can be clustered without losing their required quality;
- the cluster remains within dose and tissue tolerance;
- the intervening periods are actually lower in relevant residual demand;
- and the arrangement protects Primary work better than feasible alternatives.

It is less defensible when:

- clusters consistently exceed session tolerance;
- later priority work deteriorates;
- local tissue stress becomes excessive;
- lifestyle constraints make long sessions unreliable;
- or nominal Low periods accumulate meaningful hidden cost.

The model does not prescribe the number of High days, the number of Low days,
or a fixed same-day sequence.

## 13. Stress Organization Under Uncertainty

Stress profiles, interaction effects, and recovery time courses are often
partly uncertain.

Relevant uncertainty includes:

- incomplete actual-exposure data;
- unfamiliar interventions;
- uncertain tissue tolerance;
- changed surfaces or environments;
- weak evidence about interaction;
- noisy or noncomparable response measures;
- and limited athlete-specific history.

Uncertainty must remain attached to the demand dimension and decision it
affects.

The system should not respond to uncertainty by assuming either complete
recovery or universal incompatibility.

When the consequence of error is high, more conservative, reversible, or
previously tolerated organization may be preferable.

When consequence is limited, bounded exploratory organization can have
information value if actual exposure and response can be interpreted.

Conflicting observations should reduce confidence until differences in dose,
context, measurement, or athlete state are considered.

`Cannot Determine` is permitted when available information cannot distinguish
among materially different organization choices.

Spacing and classification should be updated from actual exposure and
evidence-weighted athlete-state estimates defined by the
[Feedback-Decision Core Model](../core_models/04_feedback_decision_model.md).

The operational handling of insufficient information follows the
[Uncertainty Handling Rules](./uncertainty_handling.md).

## 14. Stress Organization Invariants

The planning system must preserve the following rules:

1. Exercise and day labels do not uniquely define stress.
2. Actual exposure and realized demand take priority over the prescribed
   category when interpreting stress.
3. Stress is multidimensional and consequential local demand must not be hidden
   inside an unsupported total score.
4. A task can be low in one stress dimension and high in another.
5. Clustering and separation are conditional strategies with distinct benefits
   and costs.
6. Clustering is justified only when combined dose, quality, tissue stress, and
   the intended recovery opportunity remain feasible.
7. Separation can protect quality without automatically reducing total demand.
8. Recovery opportunity is relative to the prior exposure, athlete state, and
   next task; a calendar gap does not prove recovery.
9. Similar local tissue demand must be evaluated across different task labels.
10. Quality-dominant Primary work must receive the execution conditions required
    for its intended stimulus.
11. Sequence is justified by priority and task interaction, not by a universal
    exercise order.
12. Interference includes acute, residual, tissue, technical, adaptation,
    scheduling, attention, and opportunity-cost relationships.
13. The absence of proven physiological interference does not erase practical
    competition for time and recovery resources.
14. Residual cost must be evaluated against what follows, and no universal
    recovery interval applies to every exposure.
15. Competition is an actual exposure and stressor inside the planning system.
16. High / Low is an optional organization model, not a fixed frequency or
    universal law.
17. A Low label does not establish low actual cost.
18. Organization must preserve the allocation of scarce resources established
    by priority rules.
19. Unknown stress or recovery cost must not be treated as zero.
20. Uncertainty and competing explanations must remain visible when the
    evidence cannot identify one arrangement.

## 15. Rule Interfaces

[Priority Allocation Rules](./priority_allocation.md) determine which targets
and intervention roles deserve scarce resources.

Stress Organization must preserve that allocation when selected stimuli are
placed across sessions and time.

[Stimulus-Dose-Cost Core Model](../core_models/03_stimulus_dose_cost_model.md)
supplies intervention identity, dose, expected adaptation, cost, risk,
interaction, and temporal context.

[Plan Core Model](../core_models/01_plan_model.md) supplies the planning
hierarchy in which organization is represented.

[100m Performance Core Model](../core_models/02_100m_performance_model.md)
supplies the terminal criterion and performance problem that make quality and
competition expression relevant.

[Feedback-Decision Core Model](../core_models/04_feedback_decision_model.md)
supplies actual exposure, observed response, athlete-state update, and decision
scope for later revision.

[Conditional Appropriateness Rules](./conditional_appropriateness.md),
[Constraint Handling Rules](./constraint_handling.md), and
[Plan Comparison Rules](./plan_comparison.md) govern fit, feasibility, and
comparison among alternative organizations.

[Progression Rules](./progression.md) and
[Stage Transition Rules](./stage_transition.md) govern operational changes after
response or phase evidence justifies them.

Domain files may describe task-specific stress characteristics.

Workflows may order the construction or audit of a week or plan.

This Rule does not define an exercise catalogue, a fixed High / Low week,
universal spacing intervals, dose thresholds, readiness algorithms, or an
ordered planning procedure.
